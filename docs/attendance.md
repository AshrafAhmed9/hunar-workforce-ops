# Attendance without smartphones

Calling 1,000 workers every morning is the wrong primitive. It is expensive, creates a queue at exactly the busiest time of day, and turns a simple attendance signal into a high-failure outbound-dialling campaign. The unit that can resolve most disagreements is the site, not the individual worker: 100 locations means roughly 100 supervisor confirmations each day.

The primary path is a missed call from a worker's registered SIM at the site gate. A missed call works on feature phones, costs the worker nothing, and produces a timestamped phone-number signal. The system immediately calls that SIM back and asks a short confirmation in the worker's chosen language. A Hindi prompt can ask, “क्या आप आज व्हाइटफील्ड गोदाम में काम के लिए आए हैं?” The response is structured as present, absent, late, or needs-review. For people without a phone, the supervisor uses a roster roll-call. A supervisor also receives an exception list rather than a request to re-enter the entire attendance register.

This is deliberately layered. SIM binding establishes a useful identity factor but is not proof of physical presence. The callback, rather than the original missed call, reduces trivial spoofing. The system limits valid check-ins to a site’s shift window, occasionally asks a randomized roster-specific challenge, and flags a SIM that repeatedly confirms multiple people. Gate staff or supervisors resolve mismatches. A worker can correct a mistake through the exception flow; automated absence must never directly drive pay or discipline.

At 1,000 workers, a 20-second callback creates about 333 talk-minutes per day. At 30 concurrent calls that is about 12 minutes of peak capacity, before retries. A 15% failure or retry rate adds approximately 50 minutes. In contrast, 100 supervisor calls at two minutes each use 200 talk-minutes and keep the human effort focused on exceptions. A practical deployment uses the missed-call path for the majority, one supervisor confirmation per site as a reconciliation control, and a three-person operations desk that works a queue of roughly 5–10% ambiguous records (50–100 cases). The exact per-minute cost depends on the telecom contract; the model should price completed callback minutes plus retry allowance, not assume every attempted call connects.

An LLM helps with local-language speech handling, normalizing free-form answers, and summarizing the exception queue for a supervisor. It must not decide whether a person is truly at work, infer consent, override the SIM binding, or issue employment consequences. Those are deterministic policy and human-review decisions. Every callback, extracted response, override, and supervisor confirmation belongs in an auditable ledger with source, timestamp, and recording reference where policy permits.

The prototype in this repository represents that ledger: site roster, confirmed headcount, exceptions, and the bounded Hindi roll-call result fields. It intentionally does not pretend that a polished exception-queue UI makes the identity and labour-policy problem disappear.
